The 80-cent happy hour

Winey Opines · 24 September 2026

Short answer: the question is why a glass of wine in a restaurant costs what it does, and this week even Wine Enthusiast put it to a sommelier and an importer on its podcast. My answer starts smaller than theirs, with the happy-hour price: a discount that rounds to nothing is not a discount, it is a sign. The restaurant is telling you what that glass is really worth to them, which is almost nothing, and what it is worth to you is the question they would rather you not ask. Here is how to ask it.

What a glass costs the restaurant

Start with the arithmetic nobody prints on the menu. A bottle pours five glasses. A supermarket Cabernet from one of the big California labels, the kind that sells for around nine dollars retail, costs a restaurant something like five or six dollars wholesale by the case. That is roughly a dollar a glass in liquid. The old rule of thumb in the trade is that the first glass pays for the bottle, so a nine-dollar pour of a nine-dollar wine is the rule working exactly as designed: glass one covers the bottle, glasses two through five are margin.

Now do the happy hour. Nine dollars regular, eight dollars and twenty cents from four to six. Eighty cents off is a nine percent discount on the sticker and a discount of nothing at all on the wine, because the wine was never the cost. The price of that glass is the room, the pour, the server and the fact that you are sitting down. Happy hour is the restaurant admitting that out loud and then charging you for it anyway.

Why the glut never reached the wine list

If you have read the last two issues you know bulk wine in California is selling for a third of what it fetched in 2023, and tanks have changed hands for the price of the empty tank. None of that shows up on a wine list, and it is worth understanding why, because the reason is also the opportunity.

Restaurants do not buy grapes. They buy branded wine through distributors, on price lists that move with contracts and reputations, not with what a ton of Cabernet cost last month. A big label whose grapes got cheaper does not cut the wholesale price; it keeps the price and takes the margin. Then the restaurant prices the glass against the rest of the menu, which means against the cocktails and the beer, not against the wine’s cost. A twelve-dollar cocktail makes a nine-dollar glass of grocery Cabernet look reasonable, and that is the only comparison the list is designed to invite.

So the glut is real, and it is sitting in a tank in Lodi rather than in your glass. The place it does reach is the shelf, where the same wine is eleven dollars in one store and twenty-four across the street, and that gap is the one you can act on.

How to read a list for the honest pour

A wine list is a document written by someone who knows more than you about what everything on it cost. You are not going to out-know them. You can out-read them.

Divide the bottle by five. If a wine appears by the glass and by the bottle, the bottle price divided by five is what the glass should cost. Most lists price the glass above that number, sometimes far above. The gap is the markup for not committing. If you are two people having two glasses each, the bottle is nearly always the better deal and often the better wine, because the bottle list is where the restaurant keeps the things it is proud of.

Look at the second-cheapest bottle with suspicion. People avoid the cheapest bottle on a list because ordering it feels like a confession. Restaurants know this, and an old trick is to put the highest markup on the second-cheapest. I will not claim every list does it. I will say that in my experience the cheapest bottle is more often an honest one than the wine just above it.

Find the house wine’s real name. A house red with no producer on the list is a wine the restaurant does not want you to price. Ask what it is. If the answer is a producer you know, you can judge it. If the answer is a shrug, order something with a name on it.

Read the discount as a percentage. Two dollars off a ten-dollar glass is a real happy hour. Eighty cents off nine is a sign, and the sign says the regular price was already all margin.

Go where the wholesale is low and the quality is not. At a chain, the honest pours are usually Cava, Prosecco, a Côtes du Rhône, a Beaujolais or a dry Riesling. Those categories are cheap to buy by the case and hard to make badly at the price, so the glass has room to be fair. A “reserve” Cabernet from a label you see in every supermarket is the opposite: the highest brand premium and the least wine underneath it.

Do the corkage math. If the list is three or four times retail top to bottom, a twenty-five dollar corkage on a bottle you bought for forty is cheaper than anything on the list above sixty, and you know what you are drinking. Call first; some places are gracious about it and some are not.

What I do with this

If you are standing at a shelf, point me at the bottle and I will tell you what I think it is worth and whether the price in front of you is fair. At a table, type the wine and the price into the app, glass or bottle, and I will tell you the same thing. I do not know what the restaurant paid, but I know what the wine is, and that is usually enough to tell a fair pour from a sign.

Straight answers

Is an 80-cent happy hour ever worth it?

As a discount, no. As a glass, only if the wine was fairly priced to begin with, and a nine-dollar glass of a nine-dollar bottle was not.

Bottle or glass?

Two glasses each, bottle. One glass each, glass, unless the bottle divided by five is close to the glass price, in which case bottle anyway.

What should a glass cost relative to the bottle?

One fifth, plus a little for the pour. More than a quarter of the bottle price is a markup for indecision.

Why hasn’t the glut made restaurant wine cheaper?

Because restaurants buy brands through distributors at contract prices, and price the glass against the cocktails, not against what the grapes cost.

What is the safest good-value pour at a chain?

Sparkling from Spain or Italy, or a Rhône or Beaujolais red. Cheap to buy, hard to ruin.

Is corkage worth it?

Above about sixty dollars on the list, usually yes.
