The family that sold its wine for $1.5 billion just bought it back for pocket change

Winey Opines · 16 October 2026

Short answer: the question is whether a wine is still worth its price once the giant that owns it decides it is not worth keeping, and the answer is that the bottle on the shelf today has not changed, the label has, and the label is the part you are being asked to pay for. Rosemount at $11 is fair, not a find. The one to watch is the first vintage the Oatleys make themselves.

What happened

Bob Oatley planted Rosemount in the Hunter Valley in 1969. By the 1990s the Diamond Label Shiraz, with the yellow diamond on the front, was the Australian wine that half of America learned to drink on, a $10 bottle that tasted like $15 and sold by the shipload. In 2001 the family sold the whole thing to Southcorp for close to $1.5 billion. Southcorp was swallowed by Foster’s, Foster’s spun its wine off as Treasury Wine Estates and Rosemount spent twenty years as one of 76 labels in a company whose heart belongs to Penfolds.

On Monday Treasury sold it back. Rosemount, plus two smaller labels, Ingoldby and Secret Stone, to A.G. Oatley Wines, run by Bob’s son Sandy, for a sum nobody will disclose, which in this business means small. The deal closes October 23. Sandy Oatley said he was “thrilled to be able to bring Rosemount home.” Treasury said it is cutting 76 brands down to about 30 and has now sold six of them this year, along with 170 jobs.

So a label that was worth a billion and a half at the top of the Australian boom went back to the people who built it for whatever the inventory was worth. That is not a scandal. It is what happens to a supermarket brand when the company that owns it decides to spend its money on the one bottle that still commands a price.

The three kinds of corporate wine

Every big wine company owns three kinds of label, and you can tell which one you are holding if you know what to look for.

The power brand. Penfolds, Caymus, Veuve Clicquot. The company pours its best grapes, its best winemakers and its advertising into these, because the name alone adds $20 to the price. The wine is usually good. You are paying for the name, but the name is backed.

The orphan. Rosemount since 2001. A name everyone knows, kept alive because it sells by volume, fed whatever grapes are left after the power brand takes its pick. The label says Shiraz and South Eastern Australia, which is a region the size of France and Spain together. The wine is fine. It has been fine for twenty years. Nobody at the company has thought about it since the merger.

The zombie. A label that exists only because stores reorder it. Nobody makes it so much as fills it. You can spot these because the back label names a bottler in a town you have never heard of and the vintage is whatever was in the tank.

Rosemount was an orphan. The question is whether the Oatleys turn it back into something, and the honest answer is that nobody knows yet, including the Oatleys. What I do know is that Sandy Oatley has spent the last twenty years making serious wine under his own name in Margaret River, and people who have done that do not usually buy their childhood brand back to run it into the ground.

What to do with the bottle in front of you

The Diamond Label on the shelf this month was made by Treasury. Nothing about the sale changes what is in that bottle. At $11, buy it for a weeknight with a straight face, and do not expect it to be the wine you remember from 1997. The grapes come from everywhere, the style is soft and sweet-fruited and it is built to be drunk this year. Fair price, no story.

The first vintage the Oatleys make is the one to try. It will not reach a shelf before 2028. When it does, the back label will tell you whether anything changed: a real region instead of South Eastern Australia, a vintage that is the current year, a bottler in the Hunter or Margaret River. If the label says those things and the price is still under $15, that is the find, and I will say so loudly when it happens.

The general rule, for any label that changes hands: the bottle you can buy today was made under the old owner. A sale is news about the next vintage, not this one. Do not pay more for a story that has not been bottled yet, and do not pass up a fair bottle because its owner gave up on it. Treasury gave up on Rosemount. The wine did not notice.

Straight answers

Is Rosemount still worth buying?

At $11, fair, not a find. The bottle on the shelf today was made by Treasury and nothing about the sale changed it. Weeknight wine, drink it this year.

What did the Oatleys pay to get Rosemount back?

Nobody will say, which in this business means small. They sold it to Southcorp in 2001 for close to $1.5 billion; Treasury sold it back on October 12, 2026 for an undisclosed sum, closing October 23.

How do I tell an orphan brand from a real one on the shelf?

Read the back label. A region the size of a country (South Eastern Australia, California), a bottler in a town you cannot place and a price that has not moved in a decade mean nobody at the company is thinking about the wine.

When will the Oatleys’ own Rosemount be on the shelf?

Not before 2028. When it arrives, a real region on the label, a current vintage and a price under $15 would make it worth trying.
