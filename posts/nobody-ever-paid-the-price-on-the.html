Nobody ever paid the price on the “was” sticker. Here is who pays for the $9.99 endcap.

Winey Opines · 28 October 2026

Short answer: the question is who pays for the discount when a $45 wine is suddenly $19.97, and the answer is usually nobody, because nobody ever paid $45; the sticker is the price the winery wished for and the store printed it to make you feel clever. The deal is real when three things are true: the vintage is two to four years old, the label names a place, and the same bottle sells for the “was” price somewhere else this week. That bottle exists this year more than in any year I can remember, because the wine business is drowning, and it is on the endcap because a distributor needed the warehouse back. Everything else on the endcap is theater.

The sticker

The Federal Trade Commission has a rule about “was” prices, and it is short. A former price is legitimate if it is “the actual, bona fide price at which the article was offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period of time.” Offered. Not sold. A winery that prints $45 on its price sheet and never sells a bottle at $45 has “offered” it, and every store down the chain may print “was $45” for as long as it likes.

So they do. This week on wine.com a plain red Bordeaux from the 2024 vintage is $17.97, marked “regular $50.” An Oregon Pinot is $24.97, “regular $50.” A Languedoc Pinot is $13.97, “regular $32.” None of those bottles has ever changed hands at the regular price, and everybody in the trade knows it, and the discount is the product. A store that sells a $14 wine for $14 has a $14 wine. A store that sells it for $13.97, “was $32,” has a bargain, and bargains sell.

Who is allowed to pay for the shelf

In the cereal aisle the endcap is for rent. Kellogg’s pays the supermarket for it, the payment is called a slotting fee, and the deal you see there is the deal Kellogg’s wanted you to see. Wine does not work that way, and it is one of the few places where the law is on your side. America’s tied-house rules forbid a winery or distributor from giving a retailer anything of value to push its wine, and the regulator says so in plain words: “paying or crediting a retailer for any advertising, display, or distribution service is an inducement.” The regulator also admits slotting fees are “a major issue in the marketplace,” which tells you the rule is bent. But a winery cannot write the check for the endcap the way a cereal company can.

So the wine on the endcap got there one of three ways. The store put it there on its own margin, because it has too much of it. The distributor cut its price for the month, because it has too much of it. Or the distributor sold the whole lot at a loss to be rid of it, which is called a close-out, because it had far too much of it. All three are the same sentence with a different subject, and the subject this year is everybody.

The glut’s endcap

The country drank 329 million cases of wine in 2025, two percent fewer than the year before, and the bank that watches the business closest says the market “flattened in a discounting environment” with “persistent oversupply,” and that the pipeline will not be in balance by the end of this year. Translated: the warehouses are full of wine nobody ordered, and somebody has to move it before the next vintage arrives. The somebody is the distributor, the method is the close-out, and the place is the endcap.

That is the discount that is real. A close-out is a wine with a real name and a real price history that is being sold below both because it is in the way. It looks like this: a vintage two to four years old, which means it sat; an appellation on the label, which means it was made to sell at a price; a brand you have seen at the “was” price in another store this year; and a stack, because close-outs come by the pallet. I wrote in No. 14 about Napa Cabernet turning up under store labels at $10; this is the same wine, wearing its own clothes, two aisles over.

What is not real: a vintage from this year, at any price, with a “was” sticker, because it never had time to be anything else. A label that says only California or Vin de France. A brand that exists only in that chain, which is most of the brands at the big chains now, because a wine nobody else sells has no price to compare. And “Reserve,” “Limited,” “Winemaker’s Selection,” which cost nothing to print.

The calendar

Stores know when you are coming. In the two weeks between Christmas and New Year, sparkling wine sales run about 270 percent above a normal fortnight and table wine about 47 percent. A store facing that does not need to give anything away; it needs to look like it is. Black Friday wine deals are the “was” sticker at full volume.

The real sale is the second week of January, when the store is full of what did not sell for the holidays and the distributor is full of what the store sent back. Buy the Champagne for New Year in early November, when nobody is thinking about it and the price has not been dressed up yet. Buy the Cabernet you want for the winter in January, off the endcap, with a vintage on it. Buy nothing because of a sticker.

What to do with the bottle in front of you

Three questions, in the aisle, before you believe the sign.

How old is it? Two to four years, it sat and someone wants it gone; that is your discount. This year’s vintage, there is no discount, only a sticker.

Where is it from? A place you could find on a map, Médoc, Russian River, Rioja, Willamette, is a wine that had a price. A country or a state is a wine that had a tank.

What does it sell for everywhere else? This is the only test that settles it, and it is the one the sticker is designed to stop you from doing. The “was” price is real if the same bottle is sitting at that price in another store this week. If it is not, the store made it up, legally.

Or photograph the shelf and ask me. The number I give you is what the shops are charging for that bottle, all of them, this week, not what the winery wished for; it is the one price on the aisle that nobody printed to sell you something. If my number and the sticker agree, buy the stack. If my number is the sale price, the sale is the price, and you may buy it with a straight face and no illusions.

Straight answers

Are wine sale prices real?

Often not. Under FTC rule 16 CFR 233.1 a “was” price only has to have been offered, not sold, so stores print the winery’s suggested price nobody paid. The discount is real when the same bottle sells at the “was” price elsewhere.

Can wineries pay for shelf space or endcaps?

Not legally. US tied-house rules treat paying a retailer for display as an illegal inducement. Wine endcaps are funded by the store’s margin, a distributor’s temporary price cut, or a close-out.

When is the best time of year to buy wine?

The second week of January, when unsold holiday stock and returns are cleared. Buy sparkling in early November, before holiday pricing. Black Friday is mostly reference-price theater.

What is a close-out wine?

A real wine sold below its normal price because the distributor needs the warehouse space. This year it is common: US wine sales fell to 329 million cases in 2025 and the pipeline is oversupplied. Look for a vintage two to four years old and a named region.

Is Costco a good place to buy wine?

Yes for price: Costco caps its markup at about 14 percent, far below a wine shop. The catch is selection, which is whatever was cheap in bulk that month.
